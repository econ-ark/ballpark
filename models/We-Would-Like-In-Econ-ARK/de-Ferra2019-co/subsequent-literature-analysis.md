## What the subsequent literature tells us

The largest research direction to emerge is the development of **open-economy HANK models** as a full subfield. What de Ferra, Mitman & Romei (2019) pioneered — embedding heterogeneous agents into an open-economy New Keynesian framework with foreign-currency debt — has been extended in many directions. Auclert, Rognlie, Souchier & Straub (2021) size up the real income channel of exchange rates. Acharya & Challe (2025) derive optimal monetary policy. Chen, Devereux, Shi & Xu (2023) study consumption heterogeneity in open economies. Guo, Ottonello & Perez (2020) analyze monetary policy and redistribution in open economies. Bianchi & Coulibaly (2025) provide a microfounded theory of fear of floating grounded in the same household balance-sheet mechanism. The framework is also being applied to fiscal policy: Druedahl et al. (2025) compute fiscal multipliers in small open economies with heterogeneous households, and Chen, Lazarakis & Varthalitis (2025) study debt consolidation in a Euro Area HANK model.

A second strand connects household heterogeneity to **financial crises and capital flows**. Villalvazo (2025) studies how inequality interacts with asset prices during sudden stops. Vaughn (2025) models sudden stops with heterogeneous agents. De Ferra, Mitman & Romei themselves (2021) ask why capital flows from equal to unequal countries. On the empirical side, Gyöngyösi, Rariga & Verner (2021, 2025) provide micro-level evidence from Hungary's foreign-currency debt crisis — exactly the type of episode the original paper models. 

A third strand applies the framework to **contemporary policy shocks**: Auclert, Monnery, Rognlie & Straub (2023) analyze the energy crisis, Ambrosino, Chan & Tenreyro (2026) study trade fragmentation, and Bobașu, Dobrew & Repele (2025) examine energy price shocks and inequality.

Key gaps remain. No paper has modeled **endogenous household portfolio choice** between domestic and foreign currency. **Nonlinear crisis dynamics** with occasionally binding constraints are largely unexplored in open-economy HANK. **Joint optimal policy** across monetary, fiscal, macroprudential, and capital control instruments has not been studied. And **empirical validation** across multiple countries and crisis episodes is thin — most evidence comes from Hungary alone. The field has established that household heterogeneity matters for open-economy macro; the next generation of work needs to make these models quantitatively reliable enough for policy use.

## Most important subsequent papers

1. **Acharya & Challe (2025), "Inequality and optimal monetary policy in the open economy," *Journal of International Economics***: Directly answers the normative question de Ferra et al. raised — what should central banks do when households are heterogeneous and the economy is open? Derives optimal monetary policy in an open-economy HANK setting.

2. **Bianchi & Coulibaly (2025), "A theory of fear of floating," *Journal of Monetary Economics***: Provides the microfounded theory for why emerging markets avoid flexible exchange rates, grounded in the same household balance-sheet heterogeneity mechanism that de Ferra et al. highlighted.

3. **Auclert, Rognlie & Straub (2024), "Fiscal and Monetary Policy with Heterogeneous Agents," NBER Working Paper**: The comprehensive HANK policy framework from the leading group in this literature. Sets the theoretical standard that open-economy extensions build on.

4. **Druedahl, Ravn, Sunder-Plassmann, Sundram & Waldstrøm (2025), "Fiscal Multipliers in Small Open Economies with Heterogeneous Households," *IMF Economic Review***: Extends the paradigm to fiscal policy in small open economies. Shows the framework is becoming the workhorse for policy institutions.

5. **Villalvazo (2025), "Inequality and asset prices during sudden stops," *Journal of Monetary Economics***: Connects household heterogeneity to financial crises and asset pricing in emerging markets, representing the frontier where inequality meets international finance.
